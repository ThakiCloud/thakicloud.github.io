*AI-Native Culture 02*

## A Manager's Work Is Also Subject to Automation

What happens if you order employees to use AI while managers keep working the old way? Documents and code get generated quickly, but approval and decision-making stay slow. Even if individual productivity rises, organizational performance may not improve.

The first thing that must change in an AI transition is the perspective of the people who design the work. A manager should not be someone who issues more orders, but someone who decides what needs to be solved and designs the environment so the organization can solve it.

## Technology Leaders Talk About Redesigning Work

Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella's January 2025 message about the CoreAI organization outlined agentic applications with memory, permissions, and scope of action, and a shift in how software is developed.[1] Microsoft's 2026 Work Trend Index proposes the direction that leaders redesign work and that organizations develop into learning systems.[2] These should be read with the caveat that they are a vendor's research and outlook.

The AI baseline principles Tobias Lütke of Shopify presented send the message that executives and the whole organization should not be exempt from technological change.[3] It does not mean every company should follow the same people policy. What matters is that leaders directly understand and support the change in work.

## Find the Bottlenecks in How Work Gets Done

Suppose a development team cuts feature implementation time in half using AI. If the time for security review, securing test environments, and deployment approval does not change, features may not reach customers as quickly as expected. Conversely, skipping verification can deliver defects to customers.

A manager has to see the flow from the start of work to the realization of customer value. Find and remove bottlenecks such as requests waiting, duplicate approvals, environment preparation, and rework, while automating or clarifying the controls that are truly necessary.

Google's 2025 DORA research explains that AI amplifies the strengths and weaknesses of existing organizations.[4] That is why adopting a tool alone is unlikely to improve a fragile work system.

## Performance Evaluation Must Change Too

| Old management question | New management question |
|---|---|
| How many hours did you work? | What customer problem did you solve? |
| How many documents did you write? | Did the accuracy of decisions improve? |
| How much did you use AI? | Did total cost fall while keeping quality? |
| Who made the mistake? | Which control failed, and how do we improve it? |

The number of AI uses or token consumption is not performance in itself. Observe cycle time, defect rate, rework time, customer satisfaction, and operating cost together. Rather than forcing one productivity score onto an individual, it is better to use these as material for improving the team's workflow.

## A Culture Where Managers Experiment First

When introducing a new tool, pick a small piece of work and compare it with the existing way. Set success criteria and stop conditions, then publish the experiment's results. When a failure is found, check what was lacking among the data, permissions, tools, and work design before blaming any individual. Giving members learning time and approved tools is also leadership.

Management reports generated by AI also need verification. Check the sources and assumptions behind the numbers, and for important judgments such as hiring, investment, and contracts, the responsible person must review the evidence.

In the AI era, managers do not disappear; their role changes. The leader we aim for is not the one who issues the most orders, but the one who builds a system where people and technology make better decisions together.
