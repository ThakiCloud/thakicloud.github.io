*AI-Native Culture 01*

## Before Blaming AI for Hiring Failures

A resume and a take-home assignment look excellent because AI helped produce them, yet the candidate cannot explain the work themselves. What should a company change? Banning AI feels like the simplest response. But before that, it is worth asking what we actually evaluated and what we failed to evaluate.

Fabricating experience or secretly using tools that are not allowed cannot be justified. At the same time, hiring that judges a candidate's ability by the appearance of the output alone needs improvement. These two responsibilities do not cancel each other out.

## Evaluate the Decision Process, Not Just the Artifact

A developer can be asked about the rationale behind a design and its failure conditions. A marketer can be asked about how metrics are defined and what biases exist in the data. For example, give a candidate who analyzed a conversion rate a new set of conditions: duplicate visitor counts, refund handling, and a changed observation window. What surfaces is less the ability to reread the original report and more the ability to adjust and verify the analysis under the new conditions.

In hiring, provide the same time and tool access for everyone, and define job-relevant evaluation criteria in advance. It is more appropriate to evaluate the behavior a candidate actually demonstrated than to guess whether they hid AI use.

## How Global Technology Companies Approach It

OpenAI's official interview guide distinguishes whether AI is allowed depending on the evaluation format. Evaluations that require independent problem solving coexist with those that use tools, and the default interview format is remote.[1] Anthropic also encourages AI collaboration for improving applications and preparing for interviews, but restricts use in real-time interviews and assignments unless explicitly allowed.[2]

Tobias Lütke, CEO of the Canadian company Shopify, presented AI use as a baseline expectation for work in April 2025. In an official post that October, Shopify described providing model access and coding tools, and a culture of sharing experiments.[3] This is a case of building an environment where tools can be used, not merely demanding that employees be capable.

## The Hiring Approach We Propose

| Evaluation stage | Capability to check | Evaluation environment |
|---|---|---|
| Experience verification | Real contributions and foundational knowledge | Structured questioning |
| Practical assignment | Problem definition and tool use | Approved AI use |
| Change handling | Debugging and judgment | New conditions introduced |
| Result verification | Accuracy, security, accountability | Tests and explanation |

Evaluators should not reach a conclusion from simple grammar errors or traces of AI use alone. Use job-specific criteria and give the candidate a chance to explain. The approach above is not the current hiring policy of a specific company; it is an evaluation principle we propose.

## Responsibility After Hiring

Hiring someone who uses AI well does not end the AI transition. You need approved models, internal knowledge, work tools, and a system of education and verification. A customer service study formally published in 2025 found that hourly throughput productivity rose by an average of 15% for employees supported by AI, with less experienced employees gaining relatively more.[4] This is an observation of a specific type of work, and it does not guarantee the same effect across all jobs.

We do not aim to build an organization that depends only on a few people who excel without AI. We aim for one where members grow using tools and can take responsibility for the results. Rather than taking AI away to check ability, we want to be a company that provides AI and checks the potential for expanding capability.
