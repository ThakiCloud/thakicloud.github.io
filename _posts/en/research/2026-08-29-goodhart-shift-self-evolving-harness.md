If you run an unattended agent harness or design an evaluation system, this post is for you. The current direction of agent systems is for the system to diagnose its own failures while the operator sleeps, rewrite skill descriptions and routing rules, and push the results onto the next day's traffic. The problem is that these loops almost always report progress against the very evaluation bench that drives the edits. Once you make a metric the target of optimization, it stops measuring what you intended. That is Goodhart's law. The question ThakiCloud AI Research's autonomous paper, "The Goodhart Shift," tries to answer is single: when a loop optimizes against its own evaluation bench, does its self-reported gain generalize? And what measurement protocol can answer that question every night?

## A rising score and a rising ability are two different things

Start with the structure. What is being optimized here is not the model's weights but the surface of the harness: the skill descriptions the retriever actually scores against, the synonym mappings, the routing rules. The optimizer is the loop itself, the loop that later reports the gain. Two structural facts make the danger concrete in this setup.

First, the loop rewrites the very descriptions its own retriever scores against. Rewriting a description can raise the score on queries already seen without improving routing on unseen queries. A surface-level memorization channel is open. Second, the loop's reward often excludes part of the evaluation suite. A canonical case is queries where no skill should fire at all. The gain on the rewarded piece can coexist with quiet decay on the excluded piece. ThakiCloud's earlier bandit correction study observed exactly this: the preferred configuration raised the rewarded metric, top-1, from 0.558 to 0.581, while native-query hallucination on the excluded piece climbed from 0.0 to 0.4.

The paper calls this failure mode, in which the gap between the gain on cases the loop can see and the gain on a sealed holdout it never observes widens night by night, the Goodhart Shift. Two companion concepts are defined alongside it. A case-flip is a case whose outcome changed when the surface changed: for example, whether top-1 was correct, or whether a skill fired on a native or negative query. A silent case-flip is the sharper version: a regression on the hidden holdout on a night where the loop's self-report declared success. The loop's nightly summary says "no degradation," and the hidden bench says otherwise. Self-reporting cannot structurally see this. The paper also defines churn without gain: nights where the loop edited the surface ($s_n \neq s_{n-1}$) but neither side's gain exceeded the pre-registered tolerance $\epsilon$. It is compute spent with no measurable movement, and when such nights repeat, the recorded baseline slides around inside the noise of the loop's own edits.

![Two regimes of an unattended self-evolving loop: compounding improvement versus Goodhart drift](/assets/images/posts/research/goodhart-shift-self-evolving-harness/fig1_goodhart_regimes.png)
*The train score the loop can see rises in both regimes. Only when edits transfer does the sealed holdout score follow, with a near-zero night-by-night divergence. When edits memorize the visible cases, the holdout stalls or falls and the divergence accumulates nightly. This figure is a conceptual example from the paper's analytical model, not measured data.*

The core point is that the two regimes look identical in the loop's self-report. Whether the night memorized the train set or transferred, the report is a function of train only, so it says "improved" either way. The only channel that separates "the loop improved" from "the loop overfit" is the sealed holdout. The night-by-night divergence $\delta_n$, with the silent flip count as tie-breaker, is the minimal statistic that does.

## What drives the divergence is the memorization fraction of edits

Why the divergence arises, and what controls it, is shown by the paper through a deliberately simple edit-level transfer model. Suppose $e_n$ independent edits are applied to the surface on some night $n$. Each edit is one of two kinds. A transfer edit (probability $p_n$) fixes routing quality on queries the loop has never seen, like filling a vocabulary gap that generalizes across paraphrases; it adds $+a$ to both the train-side and the holdout-side expected gain. A memorizing edit (probability $1-p_n$) fixes only the visible cases it was derived from, like copying a specific train query's phrasing into a skill description; $+a$ goes to the train side only, and 0 to the holdout side.

Under this model, the paper shows that the expected night-by-night divergence is $\mathbb{E}[\delta_n] = a\, e_n\, (1 - p_n)$. The divergence is proportional to the mass of memorizing edits among the edits. On an active night (a night the loop actually edits), an expected divergence of zero is equivalent to every edit transferring ($p_n = 1$). A night with even one memorizing edit carries a positive probability of a silent holdout flip; a night with $p_n = 1$ carries no such channel to first order. That is why the night divergence, with the silent flip count as tie-breaker, is the first-order detector of non-generalizing nights.

![Expected contribution by edit type: transfer edits versus memorizing edits](/assets/images/posts/research/goodhart-shift-self-evolving-harness/fig2_edit_contribution_structure.png)
*In the paper's edit-level transfer model, a transfer edit lifts both the train side and the sealed holdout side equally, while a memorizing edit lifts only the train side. The expected night divergence is therefore governed by the memorization fraction of edits. A conceptual example from the analytical model, not measured data.*

Three things follow. First, the divergence is not a nuisance statistic; it is the estimand itself. If the loop keeps editing a lot ($e_n$ large) and the edits drift toward targeting visible cases ($p_n$ falling), $\delta_n$ rises even while both absolute scores look healthy. It is the surface-edit analog of the "goes up, then degrades" onset that Gao et al. showed for proxy reward over-optimization in 2022, and of the Pessimism's Paradox result that conservative offline learning grows hacking damage monotonically under online adaptation. Second, the memorization channel is structurally open in a retriever loop. As earlier repair-loop work showed, a correctly applied pre-patch still fails to move some retrievals, because BM25/IDF ranking is a property of the whole corpus, so vocabulary coverage alone is not enough. In the other direction, a surface edit can be aimed so narrowly that it fits $T$ but never touches routing on unseen queries. That is exactly the $(1-p_n)$ term. Third, the excluded piece is a factory of silent flips. If the reward looks only at positive queries, edits are systematically biased toward the rewarded piece and $p_n$ on the excluded cases is lower. The excluded side flips first. This is not an abstract assumption: the top-1 rise with native hallucination going 0.0 to 0.4, seen above, is a realized instance.

## The tool that separates "improved" from "overfit": the divergence gate

The paper's core contribution is a protocol that turns the definitions above into a standing instrument for the unattended loop. A train/holdout divergence gate over the whole loop sits in the evaluation layer and has six steps.

First, sealing (Seal). Before the first night, the fixed evaluation suite $E$ is split into $T$, which the loop sees, and $H$, which is written somewhere the loop's prompt, log, and reward channels cannot read. In the paper's setting, roughly a fifth of the 63-case SRA bench, about 15 cases, is assigned to $H$. The positive, native, and negative pieces are stratified so all of them exist on the sealed side too, since a positive-only reward is exactly what would otherwise ignore them.

Second, editing (Edit). The loop runs its usual nightly pass. Nothing about the loop changes, and the gate only observes; it does not steer.

Third, scoring on a fixed path. The shipped retriever configuration and code path score $s_n$ on both $T$ and $H$ with no retraining or reweighting. The night gain and $\delta_n$ are recomputed, case flips on each side are enumerated, and silent flips (unfavorable $H$ flips on nights where the train gain is non-negative) are counted. The fixed score is essential for one reason: you must be sure the score change came from the surface edit. The bandit study's lesson is here. If you let the loop turn the fusion parameters too, it moved the score by changing the instrument instead of the artifact.

Fourth, the control. A no-edit control scores the untouched $s_{n-1}$ through the same pipeline, certifying that the scoring path itself is stable night to night. The control's movement is reported as the noise floor $\eta_n$. Thanks to this control, every score movement is guaranteed to originate in the edit.

Fifth, the gate decision. The pre-registered thresholds $\tau_\delta, \tau_f$ are set relative to $\eta_n$ and to the one-case contribution on each side, and each night one of three verdicts is issued. If $|\delta_n| \le \max(\tau_\delta, \eta_n)$ and there is no silent flip, the night's self-reported gain is approved as generalized and recorded in the baseline. If $\delta_n$ exceeds the threshold or a silent flip occurs, the night is flagged: the claimed gain is unverified, the baseline stays put, and the night's self-report is marked unverified for downstream consumption. If the surface was edited but both sides' gains are under $\epsilon$, churn without gain is recorded and counted toward the audit of the loop's compute spend.

The final step hands off to the deployment layer. Flagged nights are not auto-rolled-back; they go to the existing canary-with-rollback procedure. The two layers answer different questions: the gate asks "did the improvement generalize," and the canary asks "if not, how much traffic saw it."

![Full-loop train/holdout divergence gate: the night-by-night protocol](/assets/images/posts/research/goodhart-shift-self-evolving-harness/fig3_divergence_gate_protocol.png)
*The evaluation-layer gate seals the holdout before the first night, observes the loop's edits unchanged, scores both sides on a fixed path, sets the noise floor with a no-edit control, issues one of three verdicts each night, and hands flagged nights to the deployment-layer canary. A conceptual example diagram.*

Why the seal matters, restated. If the loop ever sees the holdout scores, even indirectly through its self-report summarizing them, $H$ becomes part of the optimization target. The memorization channel extends to the holdout, and the divergence loses its meaning. That is the bench-targeting failure mode Goodhart's law warns about and the contamination concern the evaluation-safety literature describes. The operational rule is strict: $H$ is read only by the gate, never by the loop, and it is re-sealed on a long cycle with fresh traffic-labeled cases so the sealed set itself does not age into the loop's effective training distribution.

The gate's cost is minimal: one fixed-path bench score and one no-edit control per night. If the loop's reward is bench-based, no additional model calls are added. It is a local, deterministic computation that is cheap relative to the loop's own schedule. The asymmetry is what matters: for a negligible cost, it is the only machine that separates night by night a loop whose gains actually accumulate from one whose scores merely climb.

Three pieces of statistical hygiene from earlier measurement work are built in. Denominator control: when the correct skill is renamed or removed from the catalog, the denominator of a rate metric changes and can disguise itself as "gain," so the case-count change is reported alongside the scores. Tolerance versus point estimate: with a small sealed set, a single-night $\delta_n$ is noisy, so the verdict uses $\delta_n$ against the control noise floor $\eta_n$ and a windowed path-independent cumulative divergence $\Delta_N$ rather than a point estimate. Metric symmetry: all five SRA metrics, including the hallucination rate and negative avoidance rate that a positive-only reward would exclude, enter the gate, closing the factory of silent flips from the start. A sense of scale: with $|H| \approx 15$, one sealed flip moves the holdout aggregate by roughly 6.7 points. Any lower threshold cannot be interpreted as a single-night signal.

## What this paper leaves for the company, the field, and the science

For the company. ThakiCloud runs this kind of nightly loop in production over a skill registry of roughly 2,000 entries, exactly 2,234, and this week alone the loop rewrote 11 SKILL.md files. The divergence gate turns that self-reporting optimizer, which reports all night, into a verifiable one: it says precisely which of the claimed gains is real, and it protects the recorded SRA baseline (Recall@5 $\geq$ 84.4%, top-1 $\geq$ 33.3%, zero hallucination injection) from churn-without-gain nights.

For the field. The paper gives a guardrail methodology that applies to any unattended self-modifying agent system. A loop that optimizes its own evaluator can report scores upward while ability quietly declines, burning compute and energy and eroding trust in unattended automation. The divergence test is the general machine that separates "the loop improved" from "the loop overfit."

For the science. This is the first systematic way to measure, longitudinally, how self-overfitting (Goodhart) dynamics accumulate over time in a self-evolving agent harness. The 2026 harness-design literature unifies around one move: make the harness the unit of improvement. StateM freezes the model and reaches 95.3% raw accuracy on Terminal-Bench 2.1 through harness operation at the $15 scale; LongHorizon-Harness redefines long-horizon execution as task-state management with a manage-execute-audit loop where a read-only auditor verifies the environment state before the next round; EnvHarness reshapes the static environment while preserving the original verifier; Self-Harness accepts a candidate edit only after it passes regression tests on 9 model-bench combinations. But most of that literature is one-shot and model-specific, and its verification stops at acceptance-time inspection of candidate edits. Nobody asks the longitudinal question: after N nights, does the accumulated self-reported gain still generalize? The Goodhart Shift is a loop-level, longitudinal quantity. Even if every individual edit passes acceptance testing, the surface accumulated over N nights can overfit $T$ in a way no single edit did. It is the same kind of distinction as between a model's generalization gap and per-example test error. Connected to the over-optimization and reward-hacking literature, the paper makes concrete, for a self-modifying agent loop, Gao et al.'s "push a proxy hard enough and the true objective goes up, then degrades," Pan et al.'s spontaneous hacking in iterative self-refinement (where the generator and evaluator share a model, the evaluation score rises, and true quality stalls or falls), Countdown-Code's proxy/true reward separation, the Pessimism's Paradox that conservatism grows hacking damage monotonically under online adaptation, and EvalSafetyGap's unification of bench validity, contamination, and proxy optimization into a single proxy-target divergence: it turns the abstract "Goodhart gap" into the concrete, night-level, sealed-holdout divergence an operator can run as a gate.

## What this paper does not answer

The scope is drawn honestly. This is a methodology paper: it defines the failure mode, analyzes its structure, and specifies a deployable measurement protocol. It does not report new longitudinal measurements. The measurement the protocol makes possible is outside the paper's scope; the paper claims nothing beyond the protocol and the structural analysis.

First, a single organization and a single corpus. Every setting comes from one production stack, and whether the same holds for another skill library is unverified. Second, a finite sealed set. With a holdout of about 15 cases, the single-night divergence is dominated by the granularity of one case (roughly 6.7 points), and the gate's power lies in the windowed flip count, not in the point estimate. Re-sealing from traffic mitigates this problem but does not remove it. Third, the fixed-score assumption. The gate isolates the surface's effect only while the scoring path is fixed; a loop that also adapts its own retriever parameters (the case seen in the bandit correction study) needs the correction layer's own machinery on top of the gate. Fourth, model abstraction. The edit-level transfer model is a control-quantity model for identifying the governing terms, not a fitted description of any real loop. Fifth, what the gate certifies is generalization within the distribution of $E$. It does not certify improvements on traffic absent from the bench (that is the deployment canary's job), and it does not certify that the bench remains a valid, production-quality proxy as time passes. The bench ages and the traffic drifts away, a second-order Goodhart problem, and periodic re-sealing with fresh labeled cases is the response.

Within that scope, the answer to the paper's central question is clear. In every unattended self-modifying agent system, the question "did it improve?" can be answered only by a holdout the loop has never seen. One sealed holdout, one fixed scoring path, one no-edit control, one verdict line per night. That is the entire machine.
